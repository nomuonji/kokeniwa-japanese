*A shrine is not a museum you have to perform perfectly in.*

Visitors often worry about making a mistake at a Japanese shrine. The basic sequence is simple: bow once at the {{鳥居|とりい|torii · shrine gate}}, walk slightly to the side, purify your hands, offer a coin if you wish, and take a quiet moment at the worship hall. Observation and respect matter more than theatrical precision.

## At the entrance

The torii marks a boundary. A small bow before entering acknowledges that you are stepping into a sacred space. The middle of the path is traditionally associated with the kami, so walking slightly to one side is a considerate habit. At the end, turn toward the shrine and bow again before leaving.

## Water and worship

At the {{手水|ちょうず|chouzu · ritual water purification}} basin, rinse your left hand, then your right, and use your left hand to bring a little water to your mouth without touching the ladle directly to your lips. Many shrines have changed or paused this practice for hygiene, so follow posted instructions.

At the hall, the common pattern is two bows, two claps, a quiet prayer, and one final bow. This is a convention, not a test. You can simply bow and step away if the space is crowded.

## Fortunes and charms

{{おみくじ|おみくじ|omikuji · fortune paper}} are fortunes, not guarantees. If you draw a difficult one, you may tie it at the designated place or take it home and reflect on it. {{お守り|おまもり|omamori · protective charm}} are usually connected to a purpose such as travel, study, health, or safe driving. They are not souvenirs in the same sense as a keychain.

## The honest part

Shrines are living neighborhood spaces. People come to pray for exams, weddings, health, and ordinary luck. Take photographs where allowed, avoid blocking worshippers, and remember that a beautiful gate is also someone’s daily religious environment.
