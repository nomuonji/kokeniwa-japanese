*Japanese time is often measured by what is blooming, raining, or worth eating.*

{{季節|きせつ|kisetsu · season}} is not background decoration in Japanese conversation. People ask about the weather, notice the first flowers, and change menus with the calendar. You can learn the culture through a handful of seasonal words.

## Spring and early summer

{{花見|はなみ|hanami · flower viewing}} usually means enjoying cherry blossoms with food and friends, not simply looking at a tree. In May, *shinryoku* (“fresh green”) describes the bright new leaves. Then comes {{梅雨|つゆ|tsuyu · rainy season}}, a humid stretch that has its own umbrellas, laundry complaints, and hydrangeas.

## Summer

Summer vocabulary is sensory: *atsui* (“hot”), *mushiatsui* (“hot and humid”), and *yukata* (“light cotton robe”). Fireworks are *hanabi*, literally “flower fire.” A Japanese person may ask *natsu suki?* — do you like summer? The honest answer depends on whether air conditioning is nearby.

## Autumn and winter

{{紅葉|こうよう|kouyou · autumn leaves}} is the formal word for fall colors; *momiji* often points specifically to maple leaves. Food also has a season: {{旬|しゅん|shun · seasonality}} means the time when an ingredient is at its best. In winter, *kotatsu* and hot *nabe* turn staying home into a seasonal activity. Around the winter solstice, {{冬至|とうじ|touji · winter solstice}}, people may bathe with yuzu citrus.

## A useful way to practice

Keep a one-line seasonal diary: *Kyou wa atsui* (“Today is hot”), *sakura ga saita* (“the cherry blossoms opened”), or *matsutake no shun da* (“it is matsutake season”). These sentences are small, but they train you to notice what Japanese speakers notice.

## The calendar in ordinary speech

Japanese calendars also carry social vocabulary. *Shinnenkai* is a new-year gathering, *bon* is the summer period when many people return to family graves, and *setsubun* marks the traditional boundary before spring. You do not need to participate in every event to recognize the words. A shop sign saying *hatsumode* points to a first shrine visit of the year; a menu saying *aki no味覚* promises autumn flavors.

Seasonal language can be practical too. *Kisetsu no kawari-me* means the change of seasons, often a moment when people talk about catching a cold. *Samui hi ga tsuzukimasu ne* (“the cold days continue, don’t they?”) is a natural conversation opener. It is not empty small talk: it gives two people a shared observation before they move to a more personal subject.
