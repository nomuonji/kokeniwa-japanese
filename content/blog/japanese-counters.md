*Japanese does not merely count things. It asks what kind of thing they are.*

Japanese counters, {{助数詞|じょすうし|josuushi · counter word}}, are often introduced as a list of exceptions. That makes them look crueler than they are. English also says “a sheet of paper,” “a loaf of bread,” and “a pair of shoes.” Japanese simply does this classification more consistently.

## Start with the useful ten

- **個 (*ko*)** for small, roughly countable items: *ringo futatsu* (“two apples”) is also common, but *ringo ni-ko* is precise.
- **本 (*hon*)** for long, thin things: bottles, umbrellas, bananas, and pens.
- **枚 (*mai*)** for flat things: tickets, shirts, plates, and sheets of paper.
- **匹 (*hiki*)** for small animals: cats, dogs, and fish.
- **頭 (*tou*)** for large animals, especially in formal or agricultural contexts.
- **台 (*dai*)** for machines and vehicles: bicycles, cars, and washing machines.
- **冊 (*satsu*)** for bound books and notebooks.
- **人 (*nin*)** for people, with the special forms *hitori* and *futari* for one and two people.
- **杯 (*hai*)** for cups, glasses, and bowls of liquid.
- **回 (*kai*)** for number of times.

The counter comes after the number: *san-bon* (three bottles), *yon-mai* (four tickets), *go-nin* (five people). You will notice that the sound changes. *Ichi + hon* becomes *ippon*, and *san + hiki* becomes *sanbiki*. These are sound adjustments, not new vocabulary.

## Why the sounds keep changing

Japanese counters preserve combinations that are easy to say quickly. The voiceless *h* in *hon* becomes *b* or *p* after certain numbers: *ippon*, *nihon*, *sanbon*, *yonhon*, *gohon*. There are patterns, but memorizing the small table is faster than trying to derive every form in conversation.

For learners, the best strategy is chunking. Learn *ippon / nihon / sanbon* as one rhythm, not as three grammar puzzles. When you are unsure in a shop, the staff will understand a number plus the noun, and a friendly “how many?” gesture works better than freezing.

## The human counter

People use *nin* most of the time: *san-nin desu* (“we are three people”). The first two are different: *hitori* and *futari*. At a restaurant, say *futari desu* when asked how many people are in your party. It is one of the first counters worth learning because you will use it immediately.

## Sources & further reading

One practical drill is to count objects around you in Japanese. Count bottles with *hon*, books with *satsu*, and people with *nin*. Say the number aloud, then change it: one bottle, two bottles, three bottles. The sound changes become familiar through the mouth faster than through a chart. When a counter is genuinely unknown, *hitotsu* through *tootsu* can often function as a general “one thing, two things” series in casual speech.
