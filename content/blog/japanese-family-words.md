*Japanese family vocabulary asks one quiet question: whose family are we talking about?*

Japanese has a familiar word and a humble or neutral word for many relatives. You call someone else’s mother {{お母さん|おかあさん|okaasan · mother}}, but when speaking about your own mother to an outsider, {{母|はは|haha · my mother}} is often more natural. The distinction is about viewpoint and politeness, not affection.

## The core pairs

*Otoosan / chichi* are father; *okaasan / haha* are mother. *Oniisan / ani* are older brother; *oneesan / ane* are older sister. Younger siblings usually use *otouto* and {{妹|いもうと|imouto · younger sister}} without an honorific prefix when describing your own family.

## Speaking to the person directly

Children call their parents *okaasan* and *otoosan*, and adults may continue to use those forms inside the family. A shop worker may call an older male customer *otoosan* in a warm, non-literal way, especially in a family setting. Context decides whether a word is a relationship label or a form of address.

## Children and relatives

*Musuko* is son, *musume* daughter, *kodomo* child, and *ojiisan / obaasan* grandfather / grandmother or an older man / woman depending on context. Japanese speakers often clarify the relationship with *watashi no* (“my”) or a name when ambiguity matters.

## Learn the viewpoint, not a table

Write two sentences for each relative: one about your own family and one about another person’s. For example, *haha wa Osaka ni sundeimasu* (“My mother lives in Osaka”) versus *Tanaka-san no okaasan* (“Tanaka’s mother”). This practice makes the social angle visible and prevents the common mistake of using the familiar form everywhere.
