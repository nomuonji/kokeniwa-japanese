*A restaurant is easier when you know the few sentences that move the evening along.*

Japanese menus can be beautifully specific and completely unreadable. You do not need to decode every kanji before sitting down. These phrases let you get a table, ask for help, order confidently, and leave politely.

## At the entrance

If you have a reservation, say *yoyaku shiteimasu* (“I have a {{予約|よやく|yoyaku · reservation}}”). For a walk-in, *futari desu* means “we are two,” using the people counter. The staff may ask *nanmei-sama desu ka?* — how many guests? *Sama* is a respectful suffix for customers, not something you need to copy.

If you want a recommendation, ask *osusume wa nan desu ka?* (“What do you recommend?”). A useful follow-up is *kore wa nan desu ka?* while pointing at a menu item. Pointing at the menu is normal; pointing at a person is a different story.

## Ordering and dietary needs

The simplest order is *kore o hitotsu kudasai* — “one of this, please.” Replace *hitotsu* with *futatsu* for two. When you are ready, say *chuumon onegaishimasu* (“we are ready to order”). To ask whether something contains an ingredient, use *nani ga haitteimasu ka?* (“what is in it?”).

For an allergy, be direct: *ebi arerugii ga arimasu* (“I have a shrimp allergy”). Vegetarian and vegan options are not universal, so explain what you cannot eat rather than relying on one label. If the answer is unclear, a translation app and a photo of the ingredient list are sensible tools, not a failure.

## Paying

Say *okaikei onegaishimasu* for the bill. If paying separately, ask *betsu-betsu ni dekimasu ka?* (“Can we pay separately?”). Tipping is generally not expected. At some restaurants you pay at the register; at others you pay at the table. Follow the movement of the staff and you will be fine.

## Ticket machines and small corrections

Many ramen shops and fast restaurants use a ticket machine at the entrance. Look for *shokken* (meal ticket), insert cash, press the photograph, and hand the ticket to staff. If the machine is confusing, say *sumimasen, oshiete kudasai* (“Excuse me, please show me”). Staff would rather help before you press the wrong button.

If you change your mind, *kore wa iranai desu* (“I do not need this”) is clear but direct; *sumimasen, chuumon o kaetai desu* (“I would like to change my order”) is softer. At the end, *gochisousama deshita* thanks the kitchen and the person serving you. These phrases turn ordering into a sequence of small, manageable exchanges rather than one high-pressure performance.

## Sources & further reading

Save a screenshot of the phrases you need before entering a restaurant. In a noisy room, pronunciation may be harder than pointing to text. Staff may answer in Japanese, English, or by showing a menu; all three are successful communication. The goal is not to perform a perfect dialogue. It is to make your request clear and give the other person an easy way to respond.
