*The most common question foreigners ask me, and the answer isn't yes or no.*

You have a small tattoo. You want to go to an {{温泉|おんせん|onsen · hot spring}}. Every forum tells you something different, ranging from "absolutely forbidden" to "nobody cares anymore."

Both are wrong, and the true version is more useful.

## Where the rule came from

There is **no law** banning tattooed people from hot springs. It's a house rule, and it's not about tattoos as art. It's about {{刺青|いれずみ|irezumi · tattoo (traditional)}} as an organized-crime uniform.

For most of the postwar era, full-body irezumi in Japan meant one thing to a bathhouse owner, and it wasn't self-expression. Banning tattoos was a way to keep yakuza out of a business where everyone is naked, unarmed, and can't leave quickly. Other customers took the sign as a safety guarantee.

That logic is now aimed at a Danish backpacker with a small anchor on her ankle, which is why it feels absurd — because in her case it is. But it wasn't invented to be absurd, and understanding that helps you not treat the front desk clerk as a bigot. They're enforcing a rule that predates them and mostly isn't theirs.

## What's actually changing

Inbound tourism did what decades of domestic argument didn't. Millions of visitors, a meaningful share of them tattooed, turned a house rule into a revenue problem. [The Japan Tourism Agency has published guidance encouraging facilities to reconsider blanket bans](https://thejapanode.com/guides/japan-onsen-etiquette-tattoo-guide), and a lot of them have.

Today most places fall into one of [three categories](https://www.remitly.com/blog/travel/can-you-visit-an-onsen-in-japan-if-you-have-a-tattoo/): openly tattoo-friendly, conditional (cover it, or use a private bath), and still a hard no. International hotel chains — Hilton, Hyatt, and similar — are [generally fine](https://japan-dev.com/blog/tattoo-friendly-onsen).

Whole towns have gone this way. [Kinosaki Onsen is about the most tattoo-friendly onsen town in Japan](https://japan-dev.com/blog/tattoo-friendly-onsen), with most of its public bathhouses allowing tattoos or offering a private option. [Beppu has been the most progressive onsen city](https://japan-dev.com/blog/tattoo-friendly-onsen), with its tourism board actively marketing the fact across a hundred-plus baths.

The old rule is not gone. It's just no longer the default everywhere, which is genuinely new.

## Your four actual options

**1. Go somewhere that says yes.** Directories now exist specifically for [tattoo-friendly facilities](https://www.tattoofriendlyonsen.com/). This is the least stressful path and I'd take it first. Search the specific facility name plus "tattoo" before you travel — policies change and old blog posts don't.

**2. Book a {{貸切風呂|かしきりぶろ|kashikiri-buro · private rented bath}}.** A bath your group rents alone, usually [¥1,000–3,000 an hour](https://www.remitly.com/blog/travel/can-you-visit-an-onsen-in-japan-if-you-have-a-tattoo/), and almost never subject to tattoo rules. Many ryokan also have rooms with a private open-air bath attached. More expensive, zero anxiety, and honestly nicer for a couple.

**3. Cover it.** Waterproof patches are sold at Japanese pharmacies and [work fine for small tattoos](https://www.remitly.com/blog/travel/can-you-visit-an-onsen-in-japan-if-you-have-a-tattoo/). This only works if it's genuinely small — a sleeve is not getting covered and trying looks worse than not trying.

**4. Rural, small, unstaffed.** Village baths and mountain {{露天風呂|ろてんぶろ|rotenburo · outdoor bath}} often have nobody at the desk and no sign. Enforcement is mostly other bathers' comfort. This works more often than not — but it's a gamble, and if someone objects, get out gracefully.

## The thing worth more than any of this

If you get in, here's the part that actually determines whether you're a welcome guest, and almost nobody asks about it:

- **Wash first. Completely.** Sit at the shower station, soap, scrub, rinse everything off. The bath is for soaking in clean water, not for cleaning yourself. Getting this wrong is a far bigger offense than a tattoo.
- **{{掛け湯|かけゆ|kakeyu · rinsing before entering}}** — ladle hot water over yourself before stepping in, even after washing.
- **No swimsuits.** Everyone is naked. Nobody is looking at you. This is genuinely true and takes about four minutes to believe.
- **The small towel never enters the water.** Fold it on your head or set it on the rocks.
- **No long hair in the water.** Tie it up.
- **Don't swim, splash, or talk loudly.** A {{銭湯|せんとう|sentō · public bath}} is a quiet room.
- **Don't bring your phone in.** Ever. Not for one photo.

Do all that and you're a better guest than plenty of locals.

## My honest take

I'm not going to defend the rule. Applied to a tourist with a small tattoo it's a leftover, and the country knows it's a leftover — the fact that the tourism agency had to publish guidance about it says everything.

But I'd also push back gently on the framing that Japan owes you the water. A small bathhouse in a town of four thousand people is not a policy institution; it's a business run by a couple in their seventies whose regulars have an opinion. "It's 2026" is not an argument that lands there, and arguing it at the counter has never once gotten anyone into a bath.

Go where you're welcome. There are more of those places every year, and the water is the same water.
