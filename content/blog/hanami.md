*Everything you've seen about cherry blossom season is the tatemae. Here's the party underneath.*

The image the world has of {{花見|はなみ|hanami · flower viewing}} is a woman in a kimono under a pink canopy, alone, contemplating impermanence.

The reality is a hundred office workers on plastic sheets, drinking canned highballs at 4 in the afternoon, with a guy from accounting asleep face-down under the same tree.

Both of these are real. But only one of them happens on a scale of millions, and it's not the kimono one.

## What actually happens

Hanami is an {{宴会|えんかい|enkai · a drinking party}} that takes place under trees. That's it. That's the tradition.

Companies do it. University clubs do it. Families do it. You buy convenience-store food and beer, you spread a blue tarp, you sit on the ground in a coat because early April is still cold, and you stay until dark. In big parks the trees get lit up at night — *yozakura*, night blossoms — and the drinking continues.

The blossom is the excuse, and everyone knows it. There's an old Japanese saying about this: *hana yori dango* — "dumplings over flowers." We've been making fun of ourselves for coming for the snacks since roughly forever.

## The blue tarp, and the person sitting on it

Here is the most Japanese detail in the entire event.

At popular parks, a good spot has to be claimed at dawn — [laying out a tarp from around 5–6am, sometimes the night before](https://matsurimap.app/en/guides/hanami-picnic-guide-cherry-blossom-season-japan-2026). This is called {{場所取り|ばしょとり|bashotori · saving a spot}}, and at companies it has traditionally been [assigned to the newest hire](https://nihongotomo.com/articles/ohanami-truth.en.html).

So somewhere in Ueno Park right now, in April, there is a 22-year-old in a suit who has been sitting alone on a plastic sheet since 6am, guarding four square meters of dirt, waiting nine hours for their boss to arrive and enjoy the blossoms.

It's framed as a rite of passage and a bonding thing. It is also, obviously, unpaid work. To their credit, [companies have been walking it back](https://nihongotomo.com/articles/ohanami-truth.en.html) — volunteer-only, shorter shifts, and some parks now ban unattended tarps outright. Slowly. In the way Japan changes things.

If you want to understand Japanese work culture in one image, it's not a salaryman at a desk at midnight. It's that kid on the tarp.

## When it happens, and why nobody can tell you the date

{{桜|さくら|sakura · cherry blossom}} season is short and moves. The bloom front runs from the southwest of Japan north over several weeks, and the local forecast shifts by days depending on the winter.

Roughly, Tokyo hits {{満開|まんかい|mankai · full bloom}} in the last days of March or the first week of April. Kyoto is a few days behind. Sapporo is a month behind. Full bloom lasts about **a week**, less if it rains hard or blows.

That fragility is the actual point, and it's the one bit of the poetic version that survives contact with reality. There's even a word for the end of it — {{花吹雪|はなふぶき|hanafubuki · a blizzard of petals}}, petals coming down in the wind like snow. That part genuinely is beautiful, and it is genuinely why people care, and I won't pretend otherwise just to be contrary.

But you'll be watching it while sitting on a tarp holding a beer, next to a group singing badly. That's the honne.

## How to do hanami without a company behind you

You don't need a group of twelve and a 6am tarp. As a visitor:

- **Skip the famous parks at peak weekend.** Ueno and Meguro River in full bloom on a Saturday are close to immovable crowds. Go on a weekday morning, or go at 8am.
- **Use a small local park or a riverbank instead.** Almost every neighborhood in Tokyo has cherry trees along a canal or a school wall. Nobody's there. It's the same tree.
- **Buy at a konbini or a depachika.** Beer, karaage, onigiri, a cheap sheet — that's the authentic shopping list, not a picnic hamper. (See [why the konbini is genuinely good](/blog/konbini/).)
- **Sit down.** The whole thing is a sitting-still activity. Walking past the trees taking photos is the tourist version; the local version is three hours on the ground.
- **Take your garbage with you.** Parks put out extra bins in season and they overflow by evening. Bring a bag. This is the one rule where you'll actually be judged.
- **Check the toilets first.** Unromantic, unavoidable, four-hour event, one park bathroom.

## The one that isn't a party

If you want the version from the photos, it exists, but it's at a different hour. Go to a temple or a shrine with old trees at **7am on a weekday**. Nobody has set up yet. The light is low, the ground is wet, there are three old men and a photographer.

That's the serene one. It lasts about ninety minutes a year, and then the tarps come out.

Both are hanami. I'd just rather you knew which one you were buying a plane ticket for.
