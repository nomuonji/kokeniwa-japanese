*The right greeting is less about the clock than the relationship.*

Japanese greetings, or {{挨拶|あいさつ|aisatsu · greeting}}, look simple on paper. In real life they are tiny signals: how close you are, whether you have already seen someone today, and what kind of interaction is about to happen. You do not need a perfect rulebook. You need a small set of phrases and the confidence to use them.

## The four greetings that cover most days

**{{おはよう|おはよう|ohayou · good morning}}** literally belongs to the morning, but the social meaning is broader. Coworkers may say *ohayou* when starting an afternoon or evening shift because it is their first greeting of the workday. With friends, the casual *ohayou* is fine. In a workplace, *ohayou gozaimasu* is the safer polite form.

**{{こんにちは|こんにちは|konnichiwa · hello}}** is the neutral daytime greeting. It sounds a little more formal than “hi,” which is why friends often skip it and say a name, *genki?* (“doing well?”), or nothing at all. Use it with shop staff, neighbors, teachers, and people you are meeting in a semi-formal setting.

**{{こんばんは|こんばんは|konbanwa · good evening}}** works from late afternoon into the evening. Unlike English “good night,” it is a greeting, not a farewell. When you are leaving, say *oyasumi nasai* only if the other person is going to sleep or you are in a home-like setting.

## Meeting someone for the first time

The standard pair is *hajimemashite* and *yoroshiku onegaishimasu*. {{初めまして|はじめまして|hajimemashite · nice to meet you}} means “this is the first time,” so it belongs at the beginning of an introduction. Then give your name: *Alex desu* or the more formal *Alex to moushimasu*.

{{よろしくお願いします|よろしくおねがいします|yoroshiku onegaishimasu · please treat me well}} has no single English translation. It asks for goodwill in the relationship that is beginning. You will hear it after introductions, before someone helps you, and when starting a project. Do not translate it mechanically every time; hear it as “I look forward to working with you” or “thanks in advance,” depending on context.

## Greetings are not a performance

Visitors sometimes bow so deeply and so often that the greeting becomes theatrical. A small nod, eye contact, and a clear voice are enough in most casual situations. Japanese people also do not greet every stranger on a train. Greeting the person you are interacting with matters more than broadcasting politeness to the room.

At a shop, *irasshaimase* is said by the staff; you do not need to answer with the same word. A smile, a nod, or *arigatou gozaimasu* when you leave is natural. That tiny exchange is the useful lesson: greetings are invitations to make the next moment comfortable.
