*A familiar-looking word can be the most dangerous word in the room.*

{{和製英語|わせいえいご|wasei-eigo · Japanese-made English}} describes expressions built from English but shaped by Japanese usage. They are not “bad English.” They are Japanese vocabulary wearing an English jacket. The trouble starts when you assume the jacket guarantees the meaning.

## The apartment that is not a mansion

*Manshon* usually means a relatively sturdy apartment or condominium building. It does not mean a huge private house. A smaller rental apartment may be called *apaato*. If a Japanese friend says they live in a *manshon*, picture a multi-unit building, not a marble estate.

## People and places

*Sarariman* means a salaried white-collar worker, often with an image of commuting to a company. *OL* (office lady) is an older gendered term; you may still hear it, but it is not the neutral word for every woman in an office. *Konbini* is short for convenience store, and *super* means supermarket.

## Objects that trick you

The wall outlet is a {{コンセント|コンセント|konsento · electrical outlet}}, not a consent form. A *renji* is usually a microwave, while an oven is an *oon* or *obun*. *Naifu* is a knife, but *fooku* and *supu-n* are fork and spoon. These words are perfectly ordinary in Japan; the surprise is ours.

## “Claim” and “service”

If a customer makes a {{クレーム|クレーム|kureemu · complaint}}, it is not necessarily an aggressive legal claim. It can simply be a complaint to staff. *Service* may mean a free extra, as in “the restaurant gave us a service,” not customer support. Context and facial expression do the rest.

## Work, fashion, and weekends

*Salaryman* is familiar, but *salary* is not usually used as the everyday noun for pay; *kyuuryou* or *kyuuryoubi* are more natural. *My-home* means a privately owned house, not “my home” in a general emotional sense. *Working* can mean a part-time job in entertainment, while *part* is short for part-time work. A “paper driver” (*peーパードライバー*, paper driver) has a license but rarely drives.

The safest approach is curiosity rather than correction. If a colleague says “Let’s have a meeting in the morning,” they may mean a *morning service* or special promotion depending on context. Ask what they mean, then remember the Japanese usage as its own vocabulary. English loanwords are not failed translations; they are evidence of how Japanese reshapes borrowed material for local life.
