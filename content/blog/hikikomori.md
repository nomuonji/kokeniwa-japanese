*The word gets used abroad as a curiosity. In Japan it's a household problem with a number attached.*

Every few years an English-language article rediscovers {{引きこもり|ひきこもり|hikikomori · social withdrawal}} and runs the same photo: a dim room, a teenage boy, a monitor glow, stacks of instant noodles. The framing is usually *"Japan's strange young shut-ins."*

That picture is roughly a decade and about thirty years of age out of date.

## The actual number

Japan's Cabinet Office surveyed the country and found that [about **1.46 million** people aged 15–64 are living in a state of social withdrawal](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/1751676/1-46-million-working-age-people-socially-isolated-in-japan) — [roughly 2% of that age group](https://www.asiapacific.ca/publication/2-percent-japanese-labour-force-modern-day-recluses). That's not a subculture. That's a mid-sized city, twice over.

And the definition is broader than the stereotype. It [ranges from people who go out only for groceries or a hobby, to people who essentially never leave home](https://www.enca.com/japan-govt-survey-finds-15-million-living-recluses). Someone who shops at 11pm and speaks to no one is counted. That's not a gamer in a basement. That's a lot of people you'd walk past.

## Why it happens — according to the people it happened to

The most common reason respondents gave wasn't anime, or the internet, or "kids these days." It was [**quitting a job**](https://www.enca.com/japan-govt-survey-finds-15-million-living-recluses). Then illness, then the pandemic — cited by around 18–20% depending on age band.

Read that again, because it reverses the story. The stereotype says withdrawal comes first and unemployment follows. The data says the job ended first — a burnout, a contract that ran out, an illness, a workplace that ground someone down — and the withdrawal came after.

Which makes hikikomori less an exotic Japanese syndrome and more the visible edge of things every rich country has: precarious work, long hours, mental illness with poor access to care, and no soft landing.

## The part that's genuinely Japanese

I don't want to flatten it into "it's the same everywhere," because two things here do make it worse.

**One: {{世間|せけん|seken · society, "what people think"}}.** Not "society" in the abstract — the specific, watching version. Neighbors, relatives, the parents of your classmates. A household with a withdrawn adult in it feels observed. So the family hides it, and hiding it removes the exact contact that might help.

**Two: {{迷惑|めいわく|meiwaku · trouble caused to others}}.** The deepest rule in Japanese social life is *don't be a burden*. Someone who's already stopped functioning reads every offer of help as proof they're causing trouble — so they decline it. This is the same machinery I wrote about in [gaman](/blog/gaman/): endurance is a virtue right up until it becomes a trap that keeps you from asking.

Add {{恥|はじ|haji · shame}} and you get a very efficient system for making sure nobody talks about it.

## The 80/50 problem

Because the stereotype froze at "teenager," most coverage missed the aging.

The people who withdrew in Japan's post-bubble hiring ice age in the late 1990s are now in their 50s. Their parents are in their 80s, still supporting them on a pension. Japan calls this [the **80/50 problem**](https://unseen-japan.com/japan-hikikomori-8050-elderly-parents/), and it is the actual crisis — because the pension ends when the parent does, and there is no plan after that.

That's the thing English coverage keeps missing. It isn't a story about youth. It's a story about two people quietly aging in one house, and a clock.

## What Japan gets wrong about it

Domestic discourse has its own bad habit, and it's the word {{甘え|あまえ|amae · dependence on others' indulgence}} — the accusation that someone is just being coddled. "They could work if they wanted to." It's the Japanese equivalent of "get a job," and it's just as useless.

Support has historically been aimed at pushing people back into employment fast, which for someone recovering from burnout is roughly the thing that broke them, offered again. [There's a live argument here that Japan needs to rethink the whole approach](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/commentary/2025/06/16/japan/japan-needs-to-rethink-how-it-helps-hikikomori/) — and researchers now distinguish between withdrawal that's a symptom of illness and withdrawal that isn't pathological at all, [which changes what help should even look like](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10990035/).

The word that keeps coming up in the better Japanese writing on this is {{居場所|いばしょ|ibasho · a place where you belong}} — literally "a place to be." Not a job. A place. Somewhere you're expected to show up and someone notices if you don't. The programs that work tend to start there.

## Why I wrote this one

Because "Japan's weird shut-ins" is exactly the kind of story that makes a foreign reader feel a little superior, and there's nothing in the data that earns it.

1.46 million people, mostly pushed out by work and illness, held in place by a shame rule that says asking for help is itself a burden. Your country has this too. Japan just counted it, gave it a name, and then mostly declined to talk about it.
