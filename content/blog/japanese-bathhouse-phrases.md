*The hardest part of a sento is the first five minutes. After that, the water does the teaching.*

A {{銭湯|せんとう|sentou · public bath}} is a neighborhood bathhouse. An {{温泉|おんせん|onsen · hot spring}} is defined by naturally sourced mineral water, though the two experiences can feel similar to a visitor. At either one, the sequence matters more than perfect Japanese.

## The route through the building

Pay first, remove your shoes if the entrance has lockers, and enter the correct changing room. {{脱衣所|だついじょ|datsuijo · changing room}} leads to the {{浴場|よくじょう|yokujou · bathing area}}. Wash your body before entering a tub. The little stool and bucket are for washing; keep splashing low and your towel out of the water.

If you need help, ask *koko wa doko desu ka?* (“Where is this?”) or *タオルはありますか?* (“Are towels available?”). *Shampoo arimasu ka?* asks whether shampoo is provided. Facilities vary, so asking is better than assuming.

## The quiet social contract

The bath is shared, not a swimming pool. Keep voices low, do not swim, and return buckets neatly. Long hair should be tied up. Tattoos may be accepted, covered, or restricted depending on the facility; check the current policy before you go rather than arguing at the front desk.

## Leaving well

Dry yourself lightly before returning to the changing room. Drink water afterward. *Arigatou gozaimashita* to the staff is enough. The point is not to prove you know every custom; it is to leave the next person the same clean, calm space you found.

## Words you may hear inside

*Kakeyu* is the rinse you pour over yourself before entering the tub. *Uchiyu* is the indoor bath and *rotenburo* the outdoor bath. A *mizuburo* is a cold plunge, while a *denki-buro* uses a mild electric current and is not suitable for everyone. You may see signs for *sauna*, *mizu-furo*, or *tattoo OK*; policies and facilities differ, so read the posted Japanese even if the room feels familiar.

If you need to leave the bath because the water is too hot, say *atsui desu ne* with a smile; nobody expects a visitor to endure discomfort. For a private question, ask the front desk *eigo no annai wa arimasu ka?* (“Is information available in English?”). A sento visit is a small lesson in Japanese public space: personal comfort matters, but it sits inside a shared rhythm.

## Sources & further reading

If this is your first visit, choose a smaller neighborhood bath at an uncrowded time. You can watch the sequence without feeling rushed, and the staff have more time to answer a question. Bring a small plastic bag for wet items and check whether the facility sells towels. Preparation removes the awkwardness that beginners often mistake for a language problem.
