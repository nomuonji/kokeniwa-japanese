*Japanese business manners are choreography for reducing uncertainty.*

You do not need to imitate every bow. You do need to show that you understand the relationship and the other person’s time. In a first meeting, a calm greeting and a carefully handled {{名刺|めいし|meishi · business card}} do more work than elaborate gestures.

## The opening phrase

After *hajimemashite* and your name, use *yoroshiku onegaishimasu*. If you already work with the company, the standard email or meeting greeting is *osewa ni natte orimasu* — {{お世話になっております|おせわになっております|osewa ni natte orimasu · thank you for your continued support}}. It acknowledges an ongoing relationship rather than making a literal claim about being “in someone’s care.”

## Business cards

Offer your card with both hands, Japanese side facing the recipient if appropriate. Receive theirs with both hands, look at the name, and place it neatly on the table. Do not write on it during the meeting or stuff it into a pocket immediately. In a casual international meeting, these rules soften; attention and respect remain the point.

## Seating and timing

The guest is often offered the more comfortable or farther seat, but follow the host’s direction. Arrive early enough to find the room and silence your phone. If you need to interrupt, *shitsurei shimasu* — {{失礼します|しつれいします|shitsurei shimasu · excuse me}} — gives the interruption a polite frame.

## The honest part

Formal language can hide uncertainty. If you do not understand a deadline or decision, ask for {{確認|かくにん|kakunin · confirmation}}: *mou ichido kakunin shite mo ii desu ka?* Clear questions prevent more trouble than smiling through a meeting.

## Email phrases that travel into meetings

Japanese business communication relies on set openings and closings. *Osewa ni natte orimasu* begins many messages to an existing contact. *Gohakkinai no hodo onegaishimasu* requests a response by a certain date; *go-kakunin kudasai* asks the recipient to check something. These formulas can sound stiff when translated, but they make the relationship explicit and reduce ambiguity.

When a meeting ends, *honjitsu wa arigatou gozaimashita* means “thank you for today,” and *shitsurei itashimasu* marks your departure in a more formal register. You do not need to produce every phrase perfectly. Recognizing them helps you hear where the conversation is moving: greeting, request, confirmation, and closing. That structure is the real etiquette underneath the vocabulary.
