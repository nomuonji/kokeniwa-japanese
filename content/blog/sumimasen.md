*The most-used word in Japan, and the one that confuses learners longest.*

If you learn one Japanese word before landing, most guides will tell you it's *arigatō*. I'd argue it's {{すみません|sumimasen|"excuse me / sorry / thanks"}}, because you will hear it roughly four hundred times a day and it will not behave the way your textbook said.

Textbook: *sumimasen = excuse me.*

Reality: you hold a door, and the person says sumimasen. You return a dropped glove, and they say sumimasen. Someone gives you a gift and *you* say sumimasen. Nobody is sorry. Nobody did anything wrong.

## Three jobs, one word

Sumimasen does at least three things, and the split is roughly:

1. **Excuse me / getting attention.** Flagging a waiter, squeezing past someone on a train, opening a question to a stranger. This is the safe, mechanical use — [functionally identical to "excuse me"](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/sumimasen/).
2. **A light apology.** You bumped someone, you're two minutes late, you asked a question that took someone's time. [This was the original use](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/sumimasen/) — it started as an apology word and spread out from there.
3. **Thank you.** The one that breaks learners. Someone did you a kindness, and you respond with what looks like an apology.

That third one is the whole point.

## Why thank you and sorry are the same word

The kanji verb underneath is {{済む|すむ|sumu · to be settled, finished}} — for a matter to be concluded. *Sumimasen* is the negative: **"this is not settled."**

That's the entire logic. When someone goes out of their way for you, the ledger isn't square. Saying *arigatō* closes the transaction — thanks, we're good. Saying *sumimasen* says: *you spent something on me, and I know it, and I haven't repaid it.*

So it's not a weaker thank-you. In many situations it's a [heavier one — it carries humility and an awareness of the trouble you caused](https://www.daytranslations.com/blog/sumimasen-excuse-thank-sorry-japanese/), where arigatō only carries pleasure. [Japanese holds the guilt and the gratitude in a single breath](https://medium.com/@shunmatsumoto25/sumimasen-the-one-word-that-means-sorry-thank-you-and-excuse-me-all-at-once-53750d74ce9e), and doesn't consider that a contradiction.

Once you see it, the shop clerk saying sumimasen instead of arigatō stops sounding servile. She's not apologizing for existing. She's acknowledging that you came out of your way.

## Sumimasen vs gomen nasai vs arigatō

A quick field guide, because these are not interchangeable:

- **{{すみません|sumimasen|"excuse me / sorry / thanks"}}** — the default. Strangers, staff, colleagues, anything mild. Slightly formal, always safe.
- **{{ごめんなさい|gomen nasai|"I'm sorry" (personal)}}** — more personal, more emotional, more *intimate*. You say it to friends, family, a partner. Saying it to a stranger in a shop sounds oddly childlike; saying sumimasen to your partner after a real fight sounds cold and evasive. Both errors are common in learners, and both are legible instantly.
- **{{ありがとう|arigatō|thank you}}** — warm, uncomplicated gratitude. Use it when the kindness was freely given and nobody was inconvenienced. Between friends it's better than sumimasen, which can sound like you're keeping score.
- **{{恐れ入ります|おそれいります|osoreirimasu · deeply obliged}}** — the heavy business version. You'll hear staff use it. You don't need to produce it.

If you're a visitor and remember exactly one: sumimasen. It has never once made a situation worse.

## The honest downside

I don't want to sell this as pure elegance, because there's a cost and Japanese people talk about it.

Reflexively apologizing for taking up space — for asking a question, for existing near someone — has a real weight to it. It's the audible surface of {{気を遣う|きをつかう|ki o tsukau · to be considerate}}, the constant background monitoring of whether you're inconveniencing anyone. That habit is genuinely why Japan is pleasant to move through. It's also genuinely exhausting to run all day, and it shades into the same place as [gaman](/blog/gaman/): the reflex that stops you from asking for help because asking is itself an imposition.

There's a version of politeness that's care, and a version that's anxiety. Sumimasen is used for both, and from the outside they sound identical.

## How to actually use it

- **Say it before the request, not after.** "Sumimasen —" then the question. That's what it's for: opening a small door.
- **Say it when someone helps you.** Yes, even though it looks like an apology. It reads as the more considerate response.
- **Don't over-bow it.** One small nod. Foreigners tend to add a full 45-degree bow to a word that only needs a tilt of the head.
- **In a restaurant, say it out loud.** In most Japanese places you call staff over — a clear "sumimasen!" is correct, not rude. Silently raising a hand and hoping is the thing that doesn't work.

That last one is the tip visitors thank me for most. You are allowed to be audible. It's the one context where Japan expects you to raise your voice slightly, and everyone does it.
