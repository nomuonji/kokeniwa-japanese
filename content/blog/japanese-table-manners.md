*The point of Japanese table manners is consideration, not passing an exam.*

You will hear a lot of rules about {{箸|はし|hashi · chopsticks}} in Japan. Most are useful reminders to avoid gestures associated with funerals or careless handling. They are not a trap designed for visitors. If you make a mistake, pause, say *sumimasen*, and continue.

## Before and after the meal

{{いただきます|いただきます|itadakimasu · before eating}} is said before food, often with hands together. It acknowledges the people, animals, and work involved in the meal. At the end, *gochisousama deshita* thanks the person who prepared or paid for it. Neither phrase is a religious oath; they are everyday expressions of gratitude.

## The chopstick rules that matter

Do not stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice, pass food from chopstick to chopstick, or spear food when you could pick it up. These actions resemble funeral rites. Also avoid pointing with chopsticks and do not wave them while talking. Rest them on the chopstick holder when you are not eating.

## Sharing and noodles

In an izakaya or family restaurant, shared dishes are normal. Use the clean serving chopsticks if provided. If not, ask whether people prefer you to use the unused ends; practices vary. Slurping noodles is acceptable and can help cool hot broth, but it is not a command. Quiet eating is fine too.

## When you cannot finish

Portions are often moderate, but leaving food is not automatically offensive. Allergies, dietary needs, and large portions happen. Saying *onaka ippai desu* (“I am full”) is a gentle explanation. The honest rule is simple: take what you can eat, and avoid making a performance of either finishing or refusing.

## Dishes that confuse visitors

Small bowls are often lifted toward the mouth, especially rice and soup bowls. Large plates usually stay on the table. A *shoyu* dish is for dipping, not for pouring soy sauce over everything. When eating sushi, either chopsticks or fingers may be acceptable depending on the restaurant; there is no need to announce which method you chose.

At a formal meal, courses may arrive slowly. That is part of the pacing, not a forgotten order. At a casual restaurant, you may receive a wet towel called *oshibori*. Use it for your hands, then fold it and place it to the side. These details are less important than noticing whether the table is shared, whether dishes are communal, and whether the people eating with you want to talk. Manners are successful when they make the meal easier for everyone.

## Sources & further reading

If you are unsure, watch the person who invited you rather than the most elaborate etiquette video online. Restaurants differ by region, price, and cuisine. A tiny pause before copying a gesture is better than confidently applying a rule where it does not belong. Courtesy is adaptive: thank the host, keep shared dishes tidy, and let the food remain the centre of the meal.
