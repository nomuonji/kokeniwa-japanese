*The famous silence is really a way of not making your needs someone else’s problem.*

Japanese train etiquette is often described as “be quiet.” That is true, but incomplete. The deeper rule is to keep shared space predictable. {{車内|しゃない|shanai · inside a train}} is a small room full of tired strangers; your job is to make it easier to share.

## On the platform and at the doors

Stand behind the line and let people off before boarding. In busy stations, markings show where queues form. During rush hour, leave space beside the doors so passengers can exit. If you are carrying a large suitcase, move it in front of your legs or use a luggage area when available.

## Phones, conversations, and sound

Phones are normally set to silent. Texting is fine; loud calls are not. On some trains, announcements ask passengers to avoid phone use near {{優先席|ゆうせんせき|yuusenseki · priority seats}}, especially during crowded periods. This is about avoiding disturbance and keeping medical devices safe, not about a magical danger zone.

Talking with friends is allowed. The volume is the issue. Headphones should not leak music, and videos should not become the carriage soundtrack.

## Seats and consideration

Give up a seat when someone clearly needs it, including older passengers, pregnant people, injured travelers, or a parent holding a child. You do not have to guess everyone’s health. If you are unsure, offer with a small gesture and accept the answer. The verb {{譲る|ゆずる|yuzuru · to give up a seat}} also means to yield — a useful idea for the whole train.

## A more honest view

Trains are not silent temples. School groups laugh, commuters nap, and someone occasionally eats a sandwich. Etiquette is a shared compromise, not a moral ranking. Follow the flow, keep your belongings contained, and you will already be doing the important part.

## Transfers without blocking the city

At a large station, stop beside a pillar or wall before checking your map. Looking at your phone in the middle of a moving crowd creates the exact problem etiquette is meant to prevent. If you need to ask, *sumimasen, kono densha wa Shinjuku ni ikimasu ka?* is enough. Station staff may point, draw a route, or walk you to a sign.

On escalators, stand on the locally customary side and leave the other side open where people are moving. The side differs by region, and some stations now ask everyone to stand still for safety. Do not treat a regional habit as a moral test. Notice the signs and copy the flow. A train journey works when thousands of people make these tiny adjustments without needing to discuss them.

## Sources & further reading

The most useful train phrase is often not a sentence but a station name pronounced clearly. If you miss a stop, get off safely and ask at the gate rather than trying to run between platforms. *Tsugi no eki* means “the next station”; *oriru* means “to get off.” A calm correction is part of good etiquette too. Nobody expects a visitor to know a complex network on the first ride.
