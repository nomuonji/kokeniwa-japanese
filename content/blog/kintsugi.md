*Broken pottery, gold seams, and the quote that got put on a million posts.*

You've seen the picture: a pale bowl cracked into pieces and put back together, the fault lines glowing gold. Under it, some version of the same caption — *"In Japan, broken things are repaired with gold, because the history of an object makes it more beautiful."*

It's a lovely sentence. It's also doing a lot of work that the actual craft never signed up for.

I'm Japanese, and {{金継ぎ|きんつぎ|kintsugi · golden joinery}} is one of those things I only really understood after watching how the West talks about it. So here's the honne: what kintsugi actually is, what it actually costs, and why the metaphor survives anyway.

## What kintsugi actually is

Kintsugi means "golden joinery." A broken {{器|うつわ|utsuwa · a vessel, bowl}} is put back together, and the repair is finished in gold instead of being hidden.

The glue is the part nobody mentions. It's {{漆|うるし|urushi · lacquer}} — sap from the lacquer tree, the same material behind Japanese lacquerware. Urushi is the adhesive, the putty that fills missing chips, and the base coat. [The gold is only the surface](https://www.artsy.net/article/artsy-editorial-centuries-old-japanese-tradition-mending-broken-ceramics-gold) — a dusting of powder over the last thin layer of lacquer, not gold poured into the cracks. Mixing gold through the whole repair would be absurdly expensive and structurally pointless.

Urushi is also slow, and slightly hostile. It cures in humidity rather than drying in air, so pieces sit in a damp box for **weeks** between coats. Then it's sanded flush, coated again, sanded again. A real repair runs [several months, and anywhere from about $50 to $2,500 depending on difficulty](https://www.artsy.net/article/artsy-editorial-centuries-old-japanese-tradition-mending-broken-ceramics-gold). Raw urushi will also give a lot of people a rash like poison ivy on the way there.

So: not a weekend craft, and not a metaphor a {{職人|しょくにん|shokunin · craftsperson}} is thinking about while they wait three weeks for a coat to cure.

## Why "kintsugi kits" are mostly not kintsugi

Most kits sold online use synthetic resin or epoxy with gold-colored powder. That's fine — it's a real hobby and it looks good — but it isn't the traditional technique, and it's usually **not food-safe**, which the listings don't always make loud. Traditional urushi kintsugi, once fully cured, is.

If you want the real thing, [the traditional method is documented](https://en.kogei-japonica.com/media/skills/kintsugi/) and there are urushi-based kits sold in Japan. Just go in knowing you signed up for months, not an afternoon.

## The honest bit: the metaphor isn't wrong, it's just imported

Here's where I don't want to be the Japanese person who shows up to tell you your favorite quote is fake.

The philosophy attached to kintsugi in the West — that a {{傷|きず|kizu · a scar, a flaw}} is part of the object's story — is a real reading, and it isn't a foreign invention. It grows out of the same tea-ceremony sensibility as [wabi-sabi](/blog/wabi-sabi/): the chipped bowl, the imperfect glaze, the thing that shows its age instead of pretending to be new. Repairing rather than replacing is genuinely Japanese, and it's the same instinct behind [mottainai](/blog/mottainai/).

What's imported is the **self-help framing**. In Japan, kintsugi is mostly discussed as a repair technique and a craft tradition, not as a life lesson about healing from trauma. Nobody here says "I'm going through a kintsugi phase." The bowl is a bowl. You {{直す|なおす|naosu · to repair, to fix}} it because it's a good bowl and you'd rather not throw it away.

That's the gap. Not lie versus truth — emphasis. The West kept the poetry and dropped the lacquer.

## What I actually find moving about it

If you want the version that hits me, it isn't "your scars make you beautiful."

It's that somebody decided a broken bowl was worth **three months**. Not three minutes with superglue and a hope. Three months of waiting in a damp box, sanding, waiting again — for an object that costs less than the repair.

That's the real statement. It isn't about beauty at all. It's about being worth the trouble, and about the kind of patience that is honestly getting rarer here too. Japan mass-produces and discards as enthusiastically as anywhere else now. The bowl in the photo isn't a picture of how we live. It's a picture of a standard most of us don't meet.

Which, if you want a metaphor, is a better one anyway.

## How to actually see kintsugi in Japan

- **Museums.** Tea-ceremony collections often have repaired bowls on display. Look for the gold seams on old chawan — that's the real thing, sometimes centuries old.
- **Workshops.** Several studios in Kyoto and Tokyo run half-day classes. Be aware that most short classes use a simplified or synthetic method for time reasons; ask which.
- **Antique shops.** Repaired pieces turn up in old-ceramics shops, and they're often cheaper than you'd think, because a repair lowers the market value even when it raises the character.

And if someone tries to sell you a $200 "authentic kintsugi" bowl that was made pre-broken for the aesthetic — that's a product, not a repair. Nothing was saved.
